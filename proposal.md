Policy Brief: AI Lesson Preparation Tools versus Additional Administrative Staff – Which Approach Is More Effective in Reducing Teachers’ Workload?
To: Secretary for Education, New South Wales Department of Education
Policy Context: Evaluation of the second phase of the federal Workload Reduction Fund pilot in NSW, providing recommendations for the next round of investment in reducing teachers’ workload (Australian Government Department of Education 2026b).

Executive Summary
NSW should prioritise administrative support for schools with the heaviest workload, retain NSWEduChat for lesson preparation, and trial simpler forms. These measures address who completes administrative work, how lessons are prepared, and whether tasks must be repeated. NSW reports that extra staff ease administrative pressure, while early claims about AI savings have not established which approach reduces teachers’ total weekly hours more (Australian Government Department of Education 2026a; Dizdar 2026). I recommend a staged trial in approximately 66 schools, measuring working hours, teaching quality and complete costs. The Department should expand the most effective combination only after comparing results, with particular attention to high-burden and regional schools. This approach supports teachers immediately while producing evidence for the next funding decision.

Issues and Policy Context
The federal Workload Reduction Fund cofunds state and territory pilots, with jurisdictions responsible for their design, delivery and evaluation (Australian Government Department of Education 2026b). Australian full-time lower-secondary teachers reported working 46.5 hours weekly in TALIS 2024, including 8.4 hours preparing lessons and 4.7 hours on administration; 69 per cent described excessive administration as a substantial source of stress (OECD 2025). NSW’s audit identified approximately 100 tasks creating high or very high workload (NSW Department of Education 2024). The NSWEduChat expansion has a historical total budget of A$3 million and aims to reach around 700 schools; a separate NSW administrative pilot budgeted A$2.096 million for 25.2 full-time-equivalent (FTE) staff across about 66 public schools (Australian Government and New South Wales Government 2025). Their different coverage and duration make spending totals insufficient for judging effectiveness.

Impact on tasks, employment and wages
NSWEduChat can draft teaching activities, differentiated materials and assessment criteria, but teachers must review outputs. Administrative staff can assume suitable attendance, records and operational tasks (Australian Government and New South Wales Government 2025). Technology changes task allocation; effects on labour demand also depend on productivity gains and new tasks (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019). Faster preparation alone therefore proves neither lower total hours nor fewer teaching jobs. Hiring creates administrative positions; AI may increase demand for content review and technical support. NSW lists starting teacher pay at A$90,177 annually (NSW Department of Education 2026b). Neither option changes the salary scale directly. If salaries remain constant while actual hours fall, effective hourly pay rises. Staffing and digital access differences could yield unequal benefits.

Three Policy Options and Their Evaluation
Option 1 — Continue NSWEduChat with targeted training. The Department would give public-school teachers subject-specific examples for preparing materials and marking criteria; teachers would check every output. The existing expansion targets roughly 700 schools at a historical total budget of A$3 million (Australian Government and New South Wales Government 2025). For efficiency, a UK randomised trial found 25.3 minutes saved weekly on preparation for specified science lessons, without establishing an effect on total NSW working hours (Roy et al. 2024). Wide online reach supports equity if teachers with weaker digital skills receive training. Deployment is feasible because the tool exists; ongoing platform, review and training costs require separate estimates. Teachers may redirect preparation time towards feedback rather than reduce their weekly hours, so these outcomes must be measured separately.

Option 2 — Add administrative support where workload is highest. Schools would identify transferable attendance, records and operational work; the Department would recruit and train staff to undertake it. A comparable NSW pilot planned 25.2 FTE across approximately 66 schools for Terms 2–4 of 2025, with a total budget of A$2.096 million (Australian Government and New South Wales Government 2025). Staff can directly remove non-teaching tasks, and NSW reported reduced administrative burden in an earlier pilot, although total-hour savings remain unmeasured (Australian Government Department of Education 2026a). Selecting schools by workload improves equity; small schools could share posts. Recruitment and clear duties determine feasibility, while salaries create recurring fiscal costs. Measure teacher hours saved, not just administrative hours funded.

Option 3 — Remove repeated processes and simplify forms. The Department would consult teachers and administrators, select high-burden tasks, combine duplicate fields and test approved data reuse in a limited group of the 66 schools (NSW Department of Education 2024). This could improve efficiency across school sizes, including remote schools, but depends on system coordination and appropriate handling of different student needs. Victoria budgeted A$500,000 for pilot design and feasibility; Western Australia budgeted A$1.804 million for a statewide digital-forms project (Australian Government and Victorian Government 2025; Australian Government and Western Australian Government 2025). These different projects provide scale comparisons, not a NSW implementation quote. The first-stage NSW cap would be A$500,000 for diagnosis and limited prototyping, pending detailed quotes. Before modifying systems, the Department should map existing forms and identify fields that can safely be reused. Procurement and maintenance add fiscal costs.

Proposed Measures and Implementation Plan
Prioritise Option 2, retain Option 1 and undertake a limited Option 3 prototype. In late 2026, the Department should review pilot findings and select about 66 schools by administrative burden, size and location. During Term 1 of 2027, principals should identify transferable tasks, teachers should record baseline hours, and the Department should recruit staff and specify the form prototype. In Terms 2–4, schools should deploy approximately 25.2 FTE, use NSWEduChat under teacher review, and test simpler forms. The Department’s technology team should oversee systems integration, principals should manage local delegation, and teachers should complete short weekly time diaries. An independent team should report in Term 1 of 2028 before any expansion.
Plan a preliminary A$2.596 million in new funding: A$2.096 million uses the historical NSW pilot as a same-scale benchmark, and up to A$500,000 is a proposed cap for diagnosis and limited prototyping, informed by Victoria’s design project (Australian Government and New South Wales Government 2025; Australian Government and Victorian Government 2025). These figures require updated salaries and procurement quotes; existing AI operating costs must be reported separately and included in the full cost comparison. KPIs are changes in teachers’ total weekly hours versus baseline and comparable schools, administrative and preparation time, transferred tasks, filled FTE, sampled teaching quality, and full cost per hour saved. Report results by school size and region, including task switching and work intensity (Thompson et al. 2024). Success means fewer total working hours without diminished teaching quality, not simply a change in how teachers allocate their time.

Risks and Mitigation
Unreviewed AI content could harm accuracy or privacy: teachers should review outputs and follow NSW’s approved-tool guidance (NSW Department of Education 2026a). Unclear role boundaries could shift work back to teachers; principals should record delegated tasks. Cost overruns or unequal access should trigger quarterly spending reviews and targeted support for small and remote schools. If recruiting stalls, shared administrative posts could maintain coverage while the Department adjusts its recruitment plan.
